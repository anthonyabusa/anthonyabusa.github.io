Compassion is the clear-eyed recognition of suffering together with the movement toward relieving it. Pity is the same recognition filtered through a hierarchy: someone above looks down at someone below, feels badly about it, and stops there. Both begin with registering that another person is in pain. Only one carries the structure that turns registering into doing.

The distinction is old, and it is not the kind that dissolves under examination. But it has never been measured under its own name, so this entry does something slightly awkward and says so on the way: it borrows evidence from an adjacent distinction that *has* been measured, and marks exactly where the borrowed seam does and does not line up.

## What compassion actually is

Compassion is not a feeling. It is a response structure that contains feeling without being exhausted by it. The word comes from the Latin *compati*, to suffer with: *com-*, together, and *pati*, to suffer or endure. To suffer together is not to suffer the same thing. It is to register another's suffering and orient toward it in a way that does not collapse under the weight.

Three parts hold it together. Accurate perception: seeing the suffering as it is, neither inflated into catastrophe nor minimised into nothing. Affective resonance: feeling something in response, which is the signal that the other's state matters to you. And an action tendency: the impulse to reduce the suffering, which is what separates compassion from sentiment.

Drop the third and compassion degrades into what the tradition calls pity. Drop the first and it becomes sentimentality. Drop the second and it becomes calculation. All three are necessary. None is sufficient on its own.

## What pity does instead

Pity takes the perception and the resonance and stops. In their place it adds a hierarchy: I am here, you are there, and the gap between us is part of what I am feeling. The feeling is genuine. The hierarchy is the counterfeit.

This is why pity is so hard to catch from the inside. Nothing about it feels like a failure of compassion. It feels like compassion: the perception is real, the resonance is real, and the part that is missing is the part that would have cost something.

## The evidence, and where it fits

The closest thing to a controlled test of this shape comes from Tania Singer's lab, which trained people in two different responses to the same suffering and watched what each one did to them.

> Klimecki, O. M., Leiberg, S., Ricard, M. & Singer, T. (2014). "Differential pattern of functional brain plasticity after compassion and empathy training." *Social Cognitive and Affective Neuroscience*, 9(6), 873–879. [doi:10.1093/scan/nst060](https://doi.org/10.1093/scan/nst060)

Participants were trained first in empathic resonance and afterwards in compassion, with fMRI measures taken repeatedly while they watched videos of people in distress. A separate group trained in memory served as the control.

Empathy training, and not the memory control, increased negative affect, alongside raised activity in the anterior insula and anterior midcingulate cortex, regions already associated with the experience of pain and with empathy for the pain of others. Being trained to resonate more accurately with suffering made people feel worse, reliably, in a way the control condition did not.

The compassion training that followed reversed it. Negative affect came back down, self-reported positive affect went up, and the brain changes it produced did not overlap with the empathy ones at all: medial orbitofrontal cortex, pregenual anterior cingulate cortex, and ventral striatum, circuitry associated with reward and affiliation rather than with pain.

An earlier study from the same group had already shown the positive half on its own:

> Klimecki, O. M., Leiberg, S., Lamm, C. & Singer, T. (2013). "Functional neural plasticity and associated changes in positive affect after compassion training." *Cerebral Cortex*, 23(7), 1552–1561. [doi:10.1093/cercor/bhs142](https://doi.org/10.1093/cercor/bhs142)

Participants who reacted with negative affect before training reported *positive* affective experiences after compassion training, in response to the same footage of people in distress. Not indifference to the distress (they still registered it) but a different response built on top of the registering.

**Where this evidence stops.** These studies contrast compassion with *empathic distress*, not with pity. The thing that defines pity, the hierarchy, was never manipulated; nobody trained a condescension condition. What the work establishes is the general shape: that a state which looks like compassion from outside can drain the person holding it, and that the difference is structural enough to show up as separate brain systems and opposite affective outcomes. It does not establish that pity is that state. Treat it as the strongest available evidence for the category, and not as a measurement of this particular pair.

The same seam shows up in the older literature the Singer work builds on, where Batson and colleagues separated empathic concern from personal distress and found the two pushed behaviour in different directions:

> Batson, C. D., Fultz, J. & Schoenrade, P. A. (1987). "Distress and empathy: Two qualitatively distinct vicarious emotions with different motivational consequences." *Journal of Personality*, 55(1), 19–39. [doi:10.1111/j.1467-6494.1987.tb00426.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-6494.1987.tb00426.x)

## What moves a person from one to the other

The training protocols are the useful part, because they imply compassion is a skill rather than a temperament.

Learning to feel someone's pain more accurately, on its own, produces distress. That is not a moral failure or a sign of weak character; it is what the 2014 data show happening to ordinary participants under a training designed to do exactly that. Exposure without structure wears people down, which is the mechanism behind a great deal of caregiver burnout.

The compassion training added something specific: generating warmth and care deliberately, directed first at people easy to feel it for and then extended outward. That addition changed the affective outcome without removing the perception. Participants still registered the suffering. They stopped being flattened by it.

The practical reading, for anyone whose work puts them in front of suffering regularly, is that the well-intentioned default (feel it more, feel it harder) is the one the evidence says degrades. The alternative is not distance.

## The shape of the distinction

Compassion and pity are not opposites. They are neighbours. Pity is what is left when compassion loses the action tendency and fills the space with a hierarchy instead. The feeling survives. The structure does not.

That is what makes the near-enemy framing worth the trouble. The far enemy of compassion is cruelty, and almost nobody has to fight the urge toward cruelty. The counterfeit is the one that takes the decade.

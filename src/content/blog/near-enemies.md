Cruelty is not what stops most people from being compassionate. Almost nobody has to fight the urge toward cruelty. What actually interrupts compassion is subtler and much better disguised: pity, which looks like compassion from the outside and feels like it from the inside, but holds the suffering person at arm's length instead of moving toward them. You can spend a decade in pity believing you are practicing compassion, and nothing in the experience will correct you.

Fifth-century Buddhist psychology had a name for this. The *Visuddhimagga*, a Theravada manual compiled by Buddhaghosa around 430 CE, gives each of the four sublime states a pair of enemies. The **far enemy** is the plain opposite: for loving-kindness, that is hatred. The **near enemy** is the counterfeit, the state that resembles the virtue closely enough to be mistaken for it: for loving-kindness, that is attached affection, the warmth that quietly wants something back. The text is explicit that the near enemy is the more dangerous of the two, precisely because it does not announce itself as a failure.

The structure is worth stating in the abstract, because it generalizes past its origin. For any quality you are trying to build, there are two ways to lose it. You can fall to its opposite, which you will notice. Or you can settle into its impostor, which you will not.

## The lineage

The idea traveled. Jack Kornfield brought it into Western vipassana teaching, where near enemies became a standard part of the vocabulary. Roshi Joan Halifax built a whole framework on it in *Standing at the Edge*, mapping five qualities she calls Edge States (altruism, empathy, integrity, respect, engagement) against the ways each one degrades: pathological altruism, empathic distress, moral distress, disrespect, burnout. Brené Brown picked it up for *Atlas of the Heart* and has called the near enemy concept a game changer for how she thinks about connection, adding pairs of her own: the near enemy of connection is control, of self-sufficiency is hyper-independence, of hope is blind optimism.

So there is a real body of writing here. What there is almost none of is research. Search the psychological literature for "near enemy" as a construct and you find essentially nothing: no scale, no validation study, no meta-analysis, no entry in the standard handbooks. For a framework this widely repeated, it has never been operationalized.

## The same shape, under other names

That gap is more interesting than it first looks, because the *structure* has been rediscovered repeatedly by people who were not reading Buddhaghosa.

Aristotle got there first in the West. The doctrine of the mean holds that a virtue sits between two vices, one of excess and one of deficiency: courage between recklessness and cowardice. That is a near enemy and a far enemy with different labels.

Positive psychology arrived independently. Ryan Niemiec's work on character strengths reframes the whole VIA taxonomy around **overuse, underuse, and optimal use**, and his 2019 paper opens by invoking the Buddha, Aristotle, and Confucius on the same point. The empirical results are striking: across studies of depression, OCD, social anxiety and academic performance, both overuse and underuse of a strength track worse outcomes, while optimal use tracks flourishing. Strengths, measured properly, behave like virtues with enemies on both sides.

Then there is the pattern that makes the whole thing click. Over and over, when psychology has studied a virtue carefully enough, it has been forced to split the construct in two.

Pride split. Tracy and Robins showed that what people call pride is two separable factors: authentic pride, which correlates with self-esteem, agreeableness and conscientiousness, and hubristic pride, which correlates with narcissism, shame and aggression. One virtue, one near enemy, statistically distinguishable. (Worth noting that the standard scales for this were seriously challenged in 2023, so the split is real but the measurement is contested.)

Empathy split, and it split in the brain. Klimecki, Leiberg, Ricard and Singer trained one group in empathic resonance and then in compassion. Empathy training increased negative affect and activity in the anterior insula and anterior midcingulate cortex. Subsequent compassion training reversed the negative affect and lit up a *non-overlapping* network: ventral striatum, pregenual anterior cingulate, medial orbitofrontal cortex. Empathic distress and compassion are not degrees of the same thing. They are different states that feel adjacent from the inside.

That is what a near enemy is, said in the language of measurement: a construct so close to the virtue that the field initially treated them as one thing, and only careful work pulled them apart.

## Where this goes

Two clarifications before the series starts, because the frameworks are not interchangeable.

First, a near enemy is not simply "too much" of the virtue. Niemiec's overuse model and Aristotle's excess both point at quantity. But pity is not an overdose of compassion; it is compassion with the distance left in. Flattery is not too much kindness; it is kindness aimed at the wrong target. The near enemy is usually a *qualitative* substitution, not a dial turned too far. Any honest treatment has to keep those separate, and most popular writing does not.

Second, the far enemy is doing less work than it gets credit for. It is the thing you would never choose, which is exactly why it rarely gets you. Its real function is as a reference point: it tells you how far the near enemy actually is from the virtue, which turns out to be measurable.

That measurement is the companion piece to this one. [The Counterfeit Virtues](/plotted/pieces/the-counterfeit-virtues) takes twenty-one virtue triads and scores them against a database of 13,915 English words rated by roughly 1,800 people who had never encountered any of this. In twenty of twenty-one cases, the near enemy sits closer to its virtue than the far enemy does. The counterfeits really are harder to tell apart, and you can put a number on how much harder.

From here the series takes them one at a time: what the virtue actually is, what its counterfeit does instead, what evidence exists for the distinction, and what tends to move a person from one to the other. The premise is that this is a practical question rather than a taxonomic one. Nobody needs help avoiding cruelty. Plenty of people could use help noticing the year they spent in pity.
